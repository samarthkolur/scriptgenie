=========================================================
SECTION 1: EXISTING SOLUTIONS
=========================================================

The existing landscape of AI-assisted script ideation and plot generation
divides into five distinct categories, each operating at a different level
of the production workflow.


Category 1: General-Purpose LLMs Used for Scriptwriting

ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are widely used by working writers for
scriptwriting tasks including outline generation, scene drafting, and
dialogue refinement. Their adoption in the screenwriting context grew
sharply following the 2023 WGA strike, after which the WGA-AMPTP agreement
explicitly permitted AI as a production aid (while prohibiting it from
receiving writing credits). These tools are open-ended language generators.
They accept constraints as natural language instructions and do not enforce
them structurally. The 2024 algorithm audit published at the ACM Conference
on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (Mahomed, Crawford, Gautam,
Friedler, and Metaxa, 2024 - "Auditing GPT's Content Moderation Guardrails:
Can ChatGPT Write Your Favorite TV Show?", DOI: 10.1145/3630106.3658932)
analyzed over 250,000 outputs from ChatGPT's content moderation endpoint
against scripts from IMDb's 100 most-watched TV shows. The study found that
ChatGPT flagged nearly 20 percent of its own generated scripts for content
violations and flagged approximately 70 percent of actual aired TV scripts -
including PG-rated material - demonstrating that general-purpose LLMs apply
a single, undifferentiated content moderation standard that has no
correspondence to any recognized content rating system.


Category 2: Dedicated Screenwriting AI Platforms

Squibler, Sudowrite, Arc Studio Pro, FinalBit, and Highland 2 represent a
more specialized tier. These platforms combine professional screenplay
formatting with AI scene suggestions, beat generation, and collaborative
editing. Final Draft 13 introduced an AI Beat Board that allows writers to
generate and expand beats directly inside the script environment without
switching between applications. Arc Studio Pro supports multi-user
real-time collaboration with AI suggestions at the scene level. None of
these platforms accept structured, multi-dimensional production constraints
- genre convention, audience age classification, production budget tier, and
regional censorship profile - as simultaneous, enforceable input parameters.
They function as writing assistants for writers who already know what they
are writing, not as constraint-aware ideation systems that help writers
determine what kind of story is producible given a specific set of
production conditions.


Category 3: AI Story and Plot Generators

Dedicated plot generation tools such as Plot Generator, Jenova AI, and
NovelAI occupy a different niche. As documented in a 2026 industry analysis
by Jenova AI (citing Storique.ai's comparative review of purpose-built
storytelling tools), most plot generators produce single-line loglines or
brief premises - not structured narrative variants. A logline provides no
act structure, no character transformation arc, no escalating stakes
architecture, and no scene-level scope guidance. These tools do not
differentiate output based on production budget tier or content rating
target. They are premise suggestion tools, not production-grounded narrative
planners.


Category 4: Academic Research Prototypes

The research frontier includes several significant systems. Dramatron
(Mirowski, Mathewson, Pittman, and Evans, 2023, CHI - "Co-Writing
Screenplays and Theatre Scripts with Language Models," DOI:
10.1145/3544548.3581225, Google DeepMind) is the most directly relevant
academic system. Dramatron uses hierarchical prompt chaining to generate
coherent scripts complete with title, character descriptions, story beats,
location descriptions, and dialogue. It was evaluated in two-hour co-writing
sessions with 15 professional playwrights and screenwriters. Its own
industry evaluators noted that Dramatron's output can be "formulaic" and
that they "wouldn't use it to write a full play" but saw value in it for
world building and creative idea generation. Critically, Dramatron accepts
only a logline as input; it has no mechanism for accepting production budget
constraints, content rating classifications, regional censorship profiles,
or audience demographic parameters. It generates a single hierarchical
script, not a set of structurally diverse variants.

StoryVerse (Wang, Zhou, and Ledo, 2024, CHI, arXiv: 2405.13042) translates
author-defined abstract plot points into detailed character actions via
LLM-based character simulation and narrative planning. It addresses
character intentionality and plot coherence but does not accept any
production-grounded constraints. HoLLMwood (Chen et al., 2024, EMNLP
Findings) uses role-playing prompts to improve screenwriting quality. The
Plug-and-Play Dramaturge (arXiv: 2510.05188, 2025) introduces a
divide-and-conquer approach for iterative narrative refinement via
collaborative LLM agents. BiT-MCTS (arXiv: 2603.14410, 2025) applies
bidirectional Monte Carlo Tree Search grounded in Freytag's Pyramid to
generate long-form fiction with improved narrative coherence. All of these
systems target narrative quality without production-grounded constraints.
None produce multiple structurally distinct plot variants under simultaneous
multi-dimensional production constraints. None are deployed as production
web applications.


Category 5: Production Planning Tools with No AI Narrative Component

Tools such as StudioBinder, Saturation.io, and dedicated film budgeting
platforms manage production planning, budget breakdown, and scheduling.
These tools operate on the assumption that a script already exists and must
be broken down for production. They do not generate narrative content and
have no AI ideation capabilities. They represent the other half of the
missing bridge: deep knowledge of production constraints with no connection
to narrative generation.


=========================================================
SECTION 2: LIMITATIONS OF EXISTING SOLUTIONS
=========================================================


Limitation 1: No Simultaneous Multi-Constraint Enforcement

Every existing tool or research system handles constraints partially or
sequentially, not simultaneously. General-purpose LLMs treat genre as a
stylistic tone instruction and handle budget, audience rating, and
censorship as vague afterthoughts that are routinely violated in generated
output. Dedicated screenwriting platforms handle formatting but accept no
structured constraint parameters at the ideation stage. Dramatron, the most
capable academic system, accepts only a logline. Research systems such as
StoryVerse and BiT-MCTS enforce narrative structural constraints (character
motivation, dramatic arc) but accept no production-grounded constraints as
first-class inputs.

The 2024 survey "On the Challenges and Opportunities in Generative AI"
(arXiv: 2403.00025) explicitly states: "the development of scalable and
generalizable techniques capable of handling the diverse and often
conflicting demands of real-world constraints remains an open challenge."
This applies with particular force to the script ideation domain, where
genre, audience, budget, and censorship constraints do not sit in
independent dimensions - they interact and conflict in ways that must be
resolved before generation begins, not patched afterward.


Limitation 2: Blunt and Undifferentiated Content Moderation That
           Does Not Map to Any Rating Framework

The Mahomed et al. (2024) ACM FAccT audit is the most important empirical
finding in this space. ChatGPT's content moderation endpoint applies a
single global harm-prevention standard that does not distinguish between a
G-rated children's film, a PG-13 action thriller, and an R-rated crime
drama. The moderation system flagged 70 percent of real aired television
scripts - content that had already passed regulatory review by the CARA
board (MPA), been broadcast by networks, and been watched by tens of
millions of viewers. The researchers found that TV maturity ratings and
specific genres (Animation, Crime, Fantasy, and others) were statistically
significantly associated with a higher probability of being flagged -
meaning the moderation system is most aggressive precisely where creative
industry content is most commonly produced.

This is a structural failure with a specific cause: LLM alignment training
is designed for general-purpose harm prevention. It was never designed to
distinguish between content that is harmful to a general audience and
content that is legally permitted for a specific rated audience. No existing
tool resolves this by mapping its content thresholds to user-selectable
recognized rating frameworks (MPA: G, PG, PG-13, R, NC-17; BBFC: U, PG,
12A, 15, 18; CBFC: U, U/A, A; FSK: 0, 6, 12, 16, 18). As a result, every
existing tool either over-censors creative content or ignores the
censorship dimension entirely.


Limitation 3: Production Budget Is Treated as a Financial Concern
           External to Narrative, Not as a Narrative Shaper

A film's production budget tier does not merely affect cost - it directly
determines the narrative scope that is producible. This is a well-documented
principle in industry practice. A micro-budget feature under $50,000 (the
SAG-AFTRA Ultra Low Budget tier) operates with deferred compensation, a
skeleton crew, and typically requires a confined single-location story with
a cast of two to five speaking roles, no exterior action sequences requiring
stunt work, no VFX, and contemporary period setting (historical period
costuming and sets are prohibitively expensive). A low-budget indie between
$50,000 and $1 million can access the SAG-AFTRA Modified Low Budget
Agreement and supports a somewhat larger cast and more diverse locations. A
mid-range indie between $1 million and $10 million operates under full guild
agreements and can support an ensemble cast, multiple locations, and limited
visual effects. A studio-level production above $10 million (and
increasingly $20 million and above for typical studio features) can support
large ensemble casts, international location shooting, and extensive
practical and digital effects sequences (Saturation.io, 2024; Tools for
Film, 2026; Medium/The Film & Script Desk, January 2025).

These distinctions translate directly into narrative parameters: location
count, cast size, scene complexity, action set piece count, and period
setting all have direct production cost implications. No existing AI
plot-generation or script ideation system accepts a budget tier as a
structured input that shapes the narrative scope of its output. Budget is
treated, if at all, as a label - not as a set of narrative bounds that are
enforced during generation.


Limitation 4: Output Homogenization - Structurally Identical Outputs
           Under Repeated Sampling from the Same Constraint Set

Multiple peer-reviewed studies document that LLMs systematically generate
homogenized narrative outputs, and that the problem is worse - not better -
under repeated sampling from constrained prompts.

The PNAS study "Echoes in AI: Quantifying Lack of Plot Diversity in LLM
Outputs" (Xu, Jojic, Rao, Brockett, and Dolan, 2025, Microsoft Research,
arXiv: 2501.00273) analyzed GPT-4 and LLaMA-3 on story generation and
introduced the Sui Generis score, the first automatic metric measuring the
uniqueness of a plot element among alternative storylines generated from the
same prompt. The study found that LLM-generated stories contain "echoed"
plot elements that repeat idiosyncratically across independent generations.

Doshi and Hauser (2024, Science Advances - "Generative AI enhances
individual creativity but reduces the collective diversity of novel content")
found that GenAI plot idea exposure increased individual story quality
ratings but reduced collective story diversity, with cosine similarity
scores rising by 10.7 percent and 8.9 percent for groups with access to one
or five AI-generated plot ideas respectively, compared to the control group.

A March 2026 paper (arXiv: 2603.14430 - "Creative Convergence or
Imitation? Genre-Specific Homogeneity in LLM-Generated Chinese Literature")
found that LLMs "adhere to rigid narrative generation paradigms" and
"cannot correctly comprehend the meanings of narrative functions," resulting
in structurally homogenized outputs even when genre-specific prompting is
applied. The "Magic, Madness, Heaven, Sin" survey (arXiv: 2604.01504, 2026)
identifies "mode collapse" as a failure mode in which repeated samples from
the same prompt converge to structurally identical narratives.

When production constraints are added to the prompt, the problem does not
improve: it worsens. The constrained search space causes the model to
converge more tightly toward the statistically dominant narrative pattern
in its training data that satisfies the constraint - producing the
single most probable story, not the set of best stories.


Limitation 5: No Structured Variant Branching at the Ideation Stage

No existing tool generates multiple structurally distinct plot variants from
the same constraint set at the pre-writing ideation stage. The workflow in
every existing tool is linear: input a prompt, receive a single output,
iterate manually. There is no mechanism for generating, comparing, and
selecting from a structured set of narratively diverse variants that all
satisfy the same constraint bundle simultaneously. The research system
closest to addressing this (Answer Set Programming for story diversity,
arXiv: 2406.00554) operates on unconstrained story generation and does not
integrate production constraints. Dramatron produces a single hierarchical
script per run. None of these tools produce the variant set that a
development executive or independent producer actually needs at the ideation
stage: multiple distinct concepts, all production-viable, that they can
evaluate and select from before committing to script development.


=========================================================
SECTION 3: THE EXACT RESEARCH GAP
=========================================================

The research gap is precisely this:

No existing system - academic research prototype or commercial product -
treats genre convention, audience demographic, production budget tier, and
regional censorship classification as simultaneous, first-class, mutually
constraining narrative parameters, and uses them to generate a structured
set of narratively and structurally diverse plot variants in a
production-ready web application.

This gap has three interlocking components that must be understood together,
because solving any one without the others does not close the gap.


Component A: Constraint Conflict Detection and Resolution Before Generation

When a writer specifies a horror film targeting a PG-13 audience on a
micro-budget with distribution planned for both the US and India, four
constraints are immediately and specifically in tension. Horror genre
conventions, codified across decades of produced films, call for violence,
sustained dread, disturbing imagery, and often psychological or physical
brutality. The MPA PG-13 classification restricts sustained intense
violence and prohibits sexual content. The micro-budget tier ($50K and
under) prohibits location-intensive action, restricts cast to two to five
speaking roles, and eliminates practical stunt and effects work. The CBFC
(Central Board of Film Certification, India) applies additional restrictions
on violent and sexually suggestive content beyond the MPA PG-13 threshold,
meaning that some content permissible under PG-13 in the US would trigger
mandatory cuts or reclassification in the Indian market.

These four constraints are not independently satisfiable in their default
forms. A system must detect these specific tensions, explain to the writer
exactly which constraint pairs conflict and why, and then either prompt the
writer to adjust constraints or apply a documented resolution strategy -
for example, shifting the horror convention from physical violence toward
psychological dread (permissible at PG-13 and acceptable to CBFC) and
confining the narrative to a single interior location (micro-budget
compliant). No existing system performs this pre-generation conflict
detection and resolution. Every existing tool either silently violates one
or more constraints or returns a refusal with no actionable guidance.


Component B: Budget-Tier-to-Narrative-Scope Translation as a Hard
           Generation Constraint

Budget tier must be translated into specific narrative scope parameters
that function as hard bounds on what the generated plot may contain - not
as advisory notes delivered after generation. The specific parameters that
must be encoded include: maximum number of distinct locations, maximum
number of named speaking characters, permitted complexity level of action
sequences, permitted period setting, permitted VFX dependency level, and
required scene economy (dialogue-driven tension escalation vs. set-piece-
driven spectacle). These parameters must constrain the generation process,
not merely decorate the output.

This translation does not require LLM reasoning; it is a structured mapping
derivable from publicly documented industry production standards (SAG-AFTRA
tier agreements, DGA low budget agreements, standard production accounting
categories). The gap is not that this mapping is unknown - experienced line
producers know it intuitively - but that it has never been formalized into
a machine-readable constraint schema and integrated with a narrative
generation system.


Component C: Structurally Diverse Variant Generation Within a Shared
           Constraint Envelope

Given the homogenization problem documented empirically by Xu et al. (2025)
and Doshi and Hauser (2024), naive sampling from a constrained prompt does
not produce meaningfully different variants. It produces the same structural
pattern with surface-level lexical variation. Structural diversity requires
a mechanism that forces each generated variant to use a demonstrably
different narrative architecture - not just different character names or
settings, but a different fundamental relationship between protagonist,
conflict, information, and time.

The mechanism for achieving this exists in established narratological
theory. Ronald Tobias's "20 Master Plots and How to Build Them" (Writer's
Digest Books, 1993) codifies twenty structurally distinct plot archetypes
validated across film and literature, from Quest and Pursuit to Forbidden
Love and Transformation. Vladimir Propp's Morphology of the Folktale (1928)
provides a formal grammar of narrative functions that underlies much
computational narratology. These frameworks provide the structural
vocabulary for specifying, as a hard generation parameter, which narrative
architecture each variant must instantiate - ensuring that variant diversity
is structural, not merely cosmetic.

The gap is the integration of this constraint-forced structural diversity
mechanism with a multi-dimensional production constraint system, producing
variants that are structurally distinct AND simultaneously compliant with
the same genre, audience, budget, and censorship constraint bundle.


=========================================================
SECTION 4: WHY THIS GAP STILL EXISTS
=========================================================


Reason 1: Disciplinary Separation with No Bridge Institution

Research in AI narrative generation (computational narratology, LLM story
generation, interactive storytelling) is published in venues such as CHI,
EMNLP, AAAI, and arXiv. Research in film production practice (budgeting,
censorship compliance, distribution strategy, guild agreements) is practiced
in the industry and published in trade press, not in academic venues. The
citation graphs of these two bodies of knowledge do not intersect. The ACM
FAccT 2024 paper by Mahomed et al. is unusual specifically because it
bridges creative industry context (TV scripts, content ratings) with AI
systems research. The budget-to-narrative-scope mapping that a line producer
performs instinctively has never been formalized as a machine-readable
schema in any academic or industry publication. No institution sits at the
intersection of AI narrative research and production finance practice and
is incentivized to solve this integration problem.


Reason 2: LLM Alignment Training Architecturally Conflicts with
         Rating-Calibrated Content Generation

The over-moderation problem documented by Mahomed et al. (2024) is not a
bug that can be fixed by prompting more carefully. It is an architectural
consequence of how LLMs are aligned. RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from
Human Feedback) and constitutional AI alignment processes train models to
apply conservative content restrictions that maximize safety across the
broadest possible user population. The result is a system whose internal
content thresholds are miscalibrated for professional creative use cases
where content permissibility varies systematically and legitimately across
rating classifications. Building a system that correctly maps content
thresholds to specific rating classifications requires a structured knowledge
layer that sits architecturally above the raw LLM and constrains its
generation space, not a different prompt. This architectural requirement is
non-trivial to design correctly across multiple international rating systems
with differing standards.


Reason 3: Constraint Conflict Requires Pre-Generation Symbolic
         Reasoning That LLMs Cannot Reliably Perform Alone

Sequential prompting of an LLM with constraint information does not detect
or resolve conflicts between constraints. The model satisfies constraints
probabilistically; it has no representation of the logical relationship
between a budget-tier scope bound and a genre convention, and it cannot
reason about what happens when a horror convention requires an element that
a PG-13 rating prohibits and a micro-budget cannot support. Resolving these
conflicts correctly requires a symbolic reasoning layer: constraints
represented as logical conditions, a conflict detection algorithm that
identifies incompatible constraint pairs, and a resolution strategy
registry that maps conflict types to narrative alternatives. Implementing
this layer requires combining symbolic planning with neural generation,
a well-known hard problem in AI (the neuro-symbolic integration challenge)
that no existing scriptwriting system has attempted in the context of
production-grounded constraints.


Reason 4: No Economic Incentive for Commercial Tool Developers

General-purpose LLM providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) have no economic
incentive to build and maintain a curated, regularly updated regulatory
knowledge base for film content classification systems across multiple
international territories. Doing so would serve a narrow professional user
segment, require ongoing legal and domain expertise to maintain, and create
liability exposure if the system produces content that violates a regulatory
standard. Dedicated screenwriting tool companies (Final Draft, Arc Studio)
focus on the workflow of writers who already have a project; the pre-
development ideation phase with structured production constraints is not
their core market. This creates a gap that is practically important but
commercially unattractive to the existing players.


Reason 5: No Evaluation Benchmark Exists for This Task

There is no publicly available benchmark for evaluating simultaneous
multi-constraint plot variant generation under genre, audience, budget,
and censorship constraints. Without a benchmark, the problem cannot be
operationalized as a measurable research contribution. This creates a
disincentive for academic researchers to work on it: it is difficult to
publish a paper demonstrating improvement on a task for which no prior
metric exists. The absence of a benchmark is both a symptom and a
perpetuating cause of the gap.


=========================================================
SECTION 5: PROPOSED INNOVATION
=========================================================

The proposed innovation is the Constraint-Aware Script Ideation Engine
(CASIE): a web application that generates structured, diverse, and
simultaneously constraint-compliant plot variants for film and television
using a three-layer architecture that separates constraint reasoning from
narrative generation.

This separation is the core architectural principle. The constraint
reasoning layer (Layers 1 and 2) uses deterministic, rule-based logic over
a curated knowledge base. The narrative generation layer (Layer 3) uses an
LLM, but only within the validated and parameterized constraint envelope.
The LLM is never asked to reason about production constraints; it is asked
to generate a narrative within bounds that have already been verified by
the symbolic layer. This separation is what makes constraint enforcement
reliable rather than probabilistic.


Layer 1: Constraint Formalization and Conflict Detection

Users input a constraint bundle through a structured web interface. The
input schema formalizes four constraint dimensions:

Genre: Primary genre selected from a predefined taxonomy (Horror, Thriller,
Drama, Comedy, Action, Sci-Fi, Romance, Documentary-style narrative, and
hybrid combinations). Optional secondary genre modifier.

Audience: Target demographic (children under 12, young adult 12-17, adult
18-plus) and content rating classification, selectable across four
internationally recognized systems:
  - MPA (US): G, PG, PG-13, R, NC-17
  - BBFC (UK): U, PG, 12A, 15, 18
  - CBFC (India): U, U/A, A
  - FSK (Germany): 0, 6, 12, 16, 18

Budget Tier: One of four tiers derived from industry-standard production
finance categories (Saturation.io, 2024; Tools for Film, 2026):
  - Micro: under $50,000 (SAG-AFTRA Ultra Low Budget equivalent)
  - Low Indie: $50,000 to $1,000,000 (SAG-AFTRA Modified Low Budget)
  - Mid Indie: $1,000,000 to $10,000,000 (full guild agreements)
  - Studio: above $10,000,000

Censorship Context: One or more distribution territory targets, encoding
regulatory restrictions specific to each territory beyond the rating
classification (for example, CBFC's known restrictions on depictions of
violence against women or drug use that may be acceptable at a comparable
MPA or BBFC level).

Before any generation call is made, the system executes a constraint
conflict detection pass. This is a deterministic rule evaluation over the
constraint knowledge base. It identifies pairs or clusters of constraints
that are structurally incompatible or in documented tension. For each
conflict, it generates a specific natural-language explanation of what the
conflict is, which constraints are in tension, and what resolution options
are available (adjust rating, adjust genre convention, adjust budget tier,
accept a documented constraint relaxation, or receive a genre-hybrid
suggestion that resolves the conflict narratively). The user resolves all
conflicts before generation proceeds. No generation call is made against an
unresolved conflicted constraint bundle. This workflow does not exist in any
current tool.


Layer 2: Narrative Scope Parameterization

The validated constraint bundle is translated into a set of narrative scope
parameters that function as hard bounds encoded in the LLM generation
prompt. Each budget tier maps to a specific scope parameter set, derived
from industry production norms:

Micro-budget scope parameters:
  - Maximum distinct locations: 3
  - Maximum named speaking characters: 5
  - VFX complexity: none permitted
  - Permitted period setting: contemporary only
  - Action sequences: dialogue-driven tension required; no stunt work
  - Required narrative economy: high (every scene must serve multiple
    story functions; no single-function scenes)

Low Indie scope parameters:
  - Maximum distinct locations: 7
  - Maximum named speaking characters: 10
  - VFX complexity: practical effects only (no digital)
  - Permitted period setting: contemporary or recent historical (within
    30 years, minimal costuming)
  - Action sequences: limited practical action permitted

Mid Indie scope parameters:
  - Maximum distinct locations: 15
  - Maximum named speaking characters: 20
  - VFX complexity: limited digital VFX permitted
  - Permitted period setting: any historical with budget allocation
  - Action sequences: moderate; one or two set pieces permitted

Studio scope parameters:
  - Narrative scope parameters relaxed to genre convention defaults

These scope parameters are derived from the curated knowledge base and
are encoded as structured fields in the LLM system prompt - not as
natural language advice. The LLM receives them as explicit numerical
bounds that it is instructed to treat as hard constraints on every element
of the narrative it generates.

Rating-to-content-threshold mappings are also encoded at this layer. For
each rating classification in each rating system, the knowledge base
stores documented permissible content thresholds (derived from publicly
available MPA and BBFC rating criteria documentation) across the dimensions
that genre conventions most commonly invoke: violence level, sexual content
level, language level, thematic darkness level, and drug use depiction.
These thresholds are encoded as generation constraints, not as advisory
notes.


Layer 3: Structurally Diverse Variant Generation

Within the validated and parameterized constraint envelope, the system
generates three to five plot variants. Each variant is assigned a distinct
narrative structure archetype as a hard generation parameter before the
LLM call is made.

The archetype vocabulary is grounded in Ronald Tobias's "20 Master Plots
and How to Build Them" (Writer's Digest Books, 1993), the most widely
applied codification of narrative structure in professional screenwriting
practice, which itself synthesizes Aristotle's three-act structure, Propp's
narrative functions, and Campbell's hero journey into actionable structural
templates. From this taxonomy, five archetypes are selected that produce
maximally distinct structural patterns while remaining applicable across the
broadest range of genre and budget combinations:

  Archetype 1 - The Crucible: Two or three characters in a confined space
  whose conflict escalates through pressure and revelation. Structurally
  optimal for micro-budget (single or double location). Example structure:
  inciting confinement, escalating revelation, forced choice, resolution.

  Archetype 2 - Ensemble Convergence: Multiple characters with independent
  arcs whose stories converge at a single event or location. Structurally
  suited to mid-range budgets. Example structure: parallel introductions,
  intersecting complications, convergence point, collective resolution.

  Archetype 3 - Non-Linear Revelation: A mystery or secret revealed through
  fragmented, non-chronological scenes. Structurally works at any budget
  tier because location count is determined by the revealed past, not
  the telling present. Example structure: present mystery, past fragments,
  re-contextualization, revelation, resolution.

  Archetype 4 - Pursuit: A protagonist chases or is chased by an
  antagonist or force, with each act raising the stakes of the pursuit.
  Budget tier determines the scale of the pursuit; at micro-budget, pursuit
  is psychological rather than physical. Example structure: inciting chase,
  escalating obstacles, apparent capture/escape, final confrontation.

  Archetype 5 - Transformation Arc: A single protagonist undergoes a
  clearly defined internal change driven by a sequence of external events.
  Structurally intimate; works at any budget tier. Example structure:
  established flaw or wound, inciting disruption, failed old behavior,
  turning point, embodied change, new equilibrium.

The archetype is assigned to each variant before the generation call.
The system prompt for each LLM call specifies: the narrative structure
archetype (with its structural blueprint), all scope parameters from
Layer 2, all content threshold parameters from Layer 2, the genre
conventions appropriate to the selected genre, and an instruction to
produce a beat-level synopsis (not a logline, not a full script) that
satisfies all parameters simultaneously.

Each generated variant is returned with: the beat-level synopsis (minimum
five plot beats), an explicit constraint satisfaction report (one sentence
per constraint dimension confirming how the variant satisfies it), and a
flag for any constraint dimension where a relaxation was applied, with
explanation.

The structural diversity between variants is therefore not a product of
stochastic sampling variation (which produces homogenized outputs as
documented by Xu et al., 2025); it is an architectural property of the
system. Each variant is forced into a different structural container before
the LLM generates its content.


=========================================================
SECTION 6: WHY THE PROPOSED SOLUTION IS UNIQUE
=========================================================

CASIE is not a recombination of features that already exist in different
tools. Its uniqueness is architectural and resides in three properties that
no existing system possesses individually, and that no system has attempted
to combine.


Unique Property 1: Pre-Generation Constraint Conflict Detection and
                  User-Facing Resolution Workflow

Every existing system - including the most capable academic prototype,
Dramatron - either ignores constraint conflicts, silently violates them
during generation, or refuses the request with a generic error. CASIE
introduces a pre-generation constraint reasoning step that explicitly
identifies, explains, and routes constraint conflicts through a user-
controlled resolution workflow. This step is implemented as deterministic
rule evaluation over a structured knowledge base, not as an LLM prompt.
It is reliably repeatable: the same constraint bundle produces the same
conflict report every time. This property does not exist in ChatGPT,
Dramatron, StoryVerse, Final Draft, Arc Studio Pro, or any other system
in the current landscape.


Unique Property 2: Budget-Tier-to-Narrative-Scope Translation as Hard
                  Generation Constraints

The insight that film budget tiers encode specific, derivable narrative
scope parameters (location count, cast size, action complexity, period
setting) and that these parameters should constrain AI-generated narrative
output - enforced as structured fields in the generation prompt, not
mentioned as tips to the writer - is genuinely new in this domain. It
bridges two bodies of knowledge (production finance practice and AI
narrative generation) that have never been systematically connected.
It enables the system to produce plot concepts that are producible, not
merely plausible. Producibility is the property that distinguishes useful
development-stage ideation from interesting but unactionable creative
content.


Unique Property 3: Narratological Archetype Assignment as Structural
                  Diversity Enforcement Mechanism

The use of formally grounded narrative structure archetypes (Tobias, 1993;
derived from Propp, 1928; Aristotle's Poetics) as hard pre-generation
parameters - not stylistic suggestions - produces structural diversity
that is not achievable through stochastic sampling or prompt variation.
This directly addresses the homogenization failure documented in Xu et al.
(2025), Doshi and Hauser (2024), and arXiv: 2603.14430, with a mechanism
specifically designed to function within a multi-dimensional production
constraint system. The archetype assignment is done programmatically by
CASIE before any LLM call; the LLM's task is content generation within
a pre-specified structural container, not structural planning. This
architectural separation is what makes the diversity mechanism reliable.

The three properties combine into a system that existing approaches cannot
replicate by adding a feature or extending a prompt: the conflict detection
requires a knowledge base and a symbolic reasoning module; the scope
parameterization requires domain knowledge of production finance standards;
and the archetype-based diversity mechanism requires that archetype
assignment precede generation, which requires architectural control over
the generation workflow that is not available in any existing commercial
tool.


=========================================================
SECTION 7: TECHNICAL FEASIBILITY
=========================================================

CASIE is technically feasible using current technology without custom
model training. The following architecture is sufficient for a
production-ready first version.


Frontend

A React or Next.js web application provides the full user interface:
structured constraint input forms with validated field types (dropdowns,
radio groups, multi-select for distribution territories); real-time
constraint conflict display as the user inputs (client-side rule
evaluation for simple conflicts, API call for complex cross-constraint
conflicts); variant output display as structured cards showing beat-level
synopsis, constraint satisfaction report, and archetype label; and
comparison view enabling side-by-side structural evaluation of generated
variants.


Constraint Knowledge Base

A curated, versioned JSON schema stored in a structured database encodes:
  - Budget-tier-to-scope-parameter mappings (four tiers, six scope
    parameters each, derived from documented industry standards)
  - Rating-classification-to-content-threshold mappings (four rating
    systems, four to five classifications each, six content dimensions
    each, derived from publicly available MPA and BBFC documentation)
  - Genre-to-convention mappings (ten genres, documenting the narrative
    elements that define genre convention)
  - Territory-to-regulatory-restriction mappings (starting with five major
    territories: US, UK, India, Germany, Australia)
  - Conflict rule set (documented pairwise and multi-constraint conflict
    conditions and their resolution options)

This knowledge base is curated by domain experts, not generated by an LLM.
It is versioned and auditable. Updates are made by human reviewers when
regulatory frameworks change.


Constraint Conflict Detection Engine

A Python backend module evaluates the incoming constraint bundle against
the knowledge base using deterministic rule evaluation. Input: a
constraint bundle (genre, rating classification, budget tier, territory
set). Output: a conflict report (list of conflict objects, each with:
constraint pair, conflict type, severity level, and resolution options).
This module has no LLM dependency. It is unit-testable, deterministic,
and fast (sub-100ms for any bundle of four to six constraints against
a knowledge base of several hundred rules).


Narrative Scope Parameterization Engine

A second Python module translates the validated constraint bundle into
a structured scope parameter object. This object is serialized and
injected as structured fields into the LLM system prompt. It is also
deterministic and LLM-independent.


LLM-Based Plot Generation

A commercial LLM API (Anthropic or OpenAI) generates each variant. For
each variant, the system constructs a system prompt that contains:
  - The assigned narrative archetype with its structural blueprint
  - The complete scope parameter object (as numbered constraints)
  - The content threshold parameters for the selected rating
  - The genre convention parameters
  - A structured output format specification (five-beat minimum synopsis
    plus per-constraint satisfaction statement)

The five variant calls can be parallelized. Estimated generation time
per variant: two to five seconds. Total variant set generation time with
parallelization: five to eight seconds.


Post-Generation Constraint Verification

After generation, a lightweight verification step extracts structured
information from each variant (location count, character count, presence
of prohibited content categories) and checks it against scope parameters.
This can be implemented as a structured extraction prompt to a secondary
LLM call, or as a regex and keyword pass for the most critical parameters.
Variants that fail verification are flagged rather than silently passed,
and the user is informed of any scope parameter that the generated variant
appears to exceed.


Infrastructure

The complete system runs as a standard three-tier web application:
React/Next.js frontend, Python/FastAPI backend, PostgreSQL database for
the knowledge base and session storage. Deployment on any major cloud
provider (AWS, GCP, Azure) with standard containerization (Docker, Kubernetes
for scaling). No GPU infrastructure is required because all computationally
intensive steps use commercial API endpoints. No custom model training is
required for a first production version.

The most technically challenging component is knowledge base design:
the constraint schema must be expressive enough to capture real-world
constraint interactions while remaining computationally tractable for the
rule evaluation engine. This is a software engineering problem of moderate
complexity, not a machine learning research problem. It is tractable with
a skilled engineering team over a three-to-six month development cycle.


=========================================================
SECTION 8: EXPECTED IMPACT
=========================================================


Impact on Independent Filmmakers and Development Executives

The most time-expensive failure mode in independent film development is
the discovery, after a full script has been written, that the concept is
unproducible at the available budget, or that it will fail certification
in the target territory, or that it requires content adjustments that
fundamentally alter the story. Industry practitioners with experience in
micro-budget and low-budget production recognize this as a chronic problem:
writers without production experience routinely write scripts that call
for five locations when the budget supports two, or for a violence set
piece that will trigger an R rating when the project's distribution target
requires PG-13. CASIE surfaces these production-viability failures at the
pre-script ideation stage - before any writing investment has been made -
reducing development cycle waste.


Impact on Writers Targeting International Co-Production

The international co-production market requires writers to simultaneously
satisfy the content requirements of regulatory bodies in multiple
territories. A project targeting simultaneous US and Indian distribution
must satisfy both MPA and CBFC standards; where these conflict, the story
must navigate toward content that is compliant with the more restrictive
standard or accept that cuts will be required for one territory. Writers
currently do this research manually, if at all. CASIE encodes this
regulatory knowledge and applies it automatically during variant generation,
producing concepts that are designed for multi-territory compliance from
the ideation stage.


Impact on Commercial Content Strategy

PG-13 films in the US averaged $131 million in box office gross in 2021
compared to $43 million for R-rated films (documented in industry data
and cited in film market analyses including Fizzymag, 2024). The ability
to target a specific rating classification at the ideation stage - and
to receive plot concepts specifically designed to satisfy that rating's
content parameters within a chosen genre - has direct commercial value
for studios and streamers making content investment decisions. A horror
film concept designed from the ideation stage to satisfy PG-13 requires
different narrative choices than one that acquires PG-13 through post-
production editing; the former is structurally more coherent and
commercially more reliable.


Impact on AI Research in Narrative Generation

CASIE creates the first deployed testbed for multi-constraint narrative
generation under simultaneous production-grounded constraints. Real usage
data from the application will generate empirical evidence about which
constraint combinations most frequently conflict, what the distribution
of conflict types looks like across genre-budget-rating combinations,
and how effectively the archetype-based diversity mechanism produces
structurally distinct variants as measured by the Sui Generis score
(Xu et al., 2025) or semantic embedding distance metrics. This data
is a research contribution in itself: it enables future academic work
on multi-constraint narrative generation to operate from real-world
problem instances rather than artificially constructed test cases.

A secondary research impact is the constraint schema itself. A versioned,
publicly documented schema mapping budget tiers, rating classifications,
and territory censorship profiles to narrative scope parameters would be
the first such artifact in either the film industry or the AI narrative
research literature. Making this schema available under an open license
after deployment creates infrastructure that future research systems can
build on.


=========================================================
SECTION 9: COMPARISON AGAINST EXISTING APPROACHES
=========================================================


vs. ChatGPT / Claude / General-Purpose LLMs

General LLMs accept constraints as natural language instructions and do
not enforce them structurally. As documented by Mahomed et al. (2024),
they apply undifferentiated content moderation that is miscalibrated
for rated creative content, flagging 70 percent of aired TV scripts.
They produce single outputs; repeated sampling produces homogenized
variants (Xu et al., 2025). They have no knowledge of production budget
tiers and do not translate budget into narrative scope parameters.

CASIE differs: it enforces constraints through a symbolic pre-generation
layer, not through LLM reasoning; it maps content restrictions to specific
recognized rating systems; it generates multiple structurally distinct
variants through archetype assignment; and it accepts production budget
tier as a first-class narrative shaper. These are architectural
differences, not prompt differences.


vs. Final Draft 13 with AI Beat Board

Final Draft AI Beat Board operates within an existing script environment
on a script that already exists in some form. It helps writers expand
beats within a scene. It accepts no production constraint parameters.
It performs no constraint conflict detection. It generates a single output
per invocation with no structural variant framework. It is a writing
tool, not a pre-development ideation system.

CASIE operates at the pre-script ideation phase, before any writing has
begun. The two tools are not in competition; they operate at different
stages of the creative process. CASIE's output is the input to the kind
of process that Final Draft supports.


vs. Dramatron (Mirowski et al., CHI 2023, Google DeepMind)

Dramatron is the most capable and rigorously evaluated academic system
for AI-assisted script generation. It accepts a logline as input and
generates a hierarchical script through prompt chaining: title, character
descriptions, story beats, location descriptions, and dialogue. It was
evaluated by 15 professional playwrights and screenwriters in two-hour
co-writing sessions. Its own evaluators reported that its output can be
"formulaic" and noted the absence of motivation for characters and logical
gaps between consecutive scenes due to parallel dialogue generation.

Dramatron's fundamental limitation relative to CASIE is its input: a
logline. A logline is the output of the constraint resolution process
that CASIE performs. Dramatron assumes the writer already knows what
kind of story to tell. CASIE helps the writer determine what kind of
story is producible given their production conditions, then generates
multiple structurally distinct variants to choose from. Dramatron
generates one script; CASIE generates multiple constrained concepts.
Dramatron has no knowledge of budget tiers, rating classifications,
regional censorship profiles, or constraint conflicts.


vs. StoryVerse (Wang et al., 2024, CHI)

StoryVerse co-authors dynamic plots by translating abstract plot points
into character actions via LLM-based character simulation grounded in
narrative planning. It addresses character intentionality, narrative
coherence, and plot-character consistency. It accepts no production-
grounded constraints. It generates a single evolving narrative, not
a set of structurally diverse variants. It is a research prototype
without a production web application.

CASIE and StoryVerse address different problems at different stages.
StoryVerse is a tool for the writing stage, extending and elaborating
a narrative that is already underway. CASIE is a tool for the pre-writing
ideation stage, identifying which narratives to pursue.


vs. Answer Set Programming for Story Diversity (arXiv: 2406.00554, 2024)

This system uses Answer Set Programming to improve story diversity in
LLM-based generation, demonstrating improved Sui Generis-type diversity
scores measured with the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence embedding model across
six different premise prompts. It addresses the homogenization problem
directly and represents genuine progress on diversity in story generation.

It does not address production-grounded constraints. It is designed for
unconstrained story premises and does not incorporate budget tiers, rating
classifications, or censorship profiles. Its diversity mechanism (symbolic
planning over narrative graph elements) is not designed to operate within
a multi-dimensional production constraint envelope. CASIE addresses the
same homogenization problem with a different mechanism (archetype
assignment as a pre-generation hard parameter) that is specifically
designed to function under simultaneous multi-dimensional production
constraints.


vs. BiT-MCTS (arXiv: 2603.14410, 2025)

BiT-MCTS applies bidirectional Monte Carlo Tree Search grounded in
Freytag's Pyramid to long-form fiction generation in the Chinese web
literature domain. It improves narrative coherence, plot structure, and
thematic depth relative to baselines. It accepts a theme as input and
generates a single structured long-form fiction. It does not address
film production constraints, budget tiers, rating classifications, or
regional censorship. It is a fiction generation system, not a script
ideation system. It is not deployed as a web application.


=========================================================
SECTION 10: RISKS AND LIMITATIONS
=========================================================


Risk 1: Knowledge Base Accuracy and Maintenance Over Time

The regulatory standards encoded in the knowledge base (MPA rating
criteria, BBFC classification guidelines, CBFC regulations, FSK standards)
are not fully codified in public documentation and are applied by human
review boards with some degree of subjectivity. The MPA's CARA board
applies rating criteria through group deliberation, and edge cases
regularly produce unexpected outcomes. Knowledge base content may not
perfectly capture this subjectivity. More critically, regulatory frameworks
evolve: the MPA has revised its rating criteria multiple times, and
international censorship boards update their standards in response to
political and cultural shifts. The knowledge base requires scheduled review
and update cycles, staffed by domain experts with current regulatory
knowledge. This is an ongoing operational cost that must be planned for.

Mitigation: Version the knowledge base explicitly; display the version
and date to users; make conflict detection logic transparent and auditable
so users can understand why a conflict was flagged; and provide a
mechanism for users to report incorrect conflict detections for review.


Risk 2: LLM Constraint Adherence Is Probabilistic, Not Guaranteed

Even with explicit scope parameters encoded in the system prompt, LLMs
do not guarantee adherence to all constraints in every generation. A
generated variant may subtly violate a scope parameter - for example,
a synopsis that implies seven locations when the budget tier permits
three, or that includes a violent scene element that exceeds the content
threshold for the selected rating. The post-generation verification step
catches the most egregious violations, but semantic nuance (an implied
location that is not explicitly named, a content element that is borderline
rather than clearly over-threshold) may not be reliably detected.

Mitigation: The post-generation verification step should be implemented
as a structured LLM extraction plus rule check, not just a keyword pass.
The user-facing output should clearly indicate the confidence level of
constraint satisfaction and encourage manual review for any variant
flagged as borderline compliant. The system should never present generated
variants as certified compliant; it should present them as
CASIE-verified-for-scope with the specific parameters checked and the
specific parameters that require user review clearly labeled.


Risk 3: Scope Is Ideation, Not Script - User Expectation Management

CASIE generates plot variants at the five-beat synopsis level. It does
not produce full screenplays, scene-level dialogue, or formatted scripts.
Users who approach the system expecting a full script deliverable will be
disappointed. This is a deliberate design choice grounded in the actual
problem being solved: pre-development ideation, the phase at which a
producer or writer determines which concepts are worth developing into
full scripts. The ideation-to-script pipeline requires human creative
investment after CASIE's output, not before it.

Mitigation: User experience design must establish this scope clearly at
every touchpoint. The product should explicitly describe itself as a
pre-development ideation tool and explain the workflow: CASIE produces
the concept; the writer develops the script. Integration guidance pointing
toward tools like Dramatron or Final Draft for the subsequent stage would
be appropriate.


Risk 4: Constraint Schema Complexity Creates Adoption Friction

The richer the constraint input schema, the more powerful the system
and the steeper the learning curve for new users. A writer unfamiliar
with production finance may not know which SAG-AFTRA tier their project
targets, may not understand the difference between MPA and BBFC rating
criteria, and may not know which distribution territories are relevant
to their project. Requiring this knowledge as input creates a barrier
that may reduce adoption among the independent writer segment.

Mitigation: The input interface must provide intelligent defaults (for
example, defaulting to US-only distribution, mid-indie budget tier, and
PG-13 rating as a starting point); contextual tooltips explaining each
constraint dimension in production terms; a simplified "quick start"
mode that asks only for genre, audience type (children/teen/adult), and
production scale (small/medium/large) and maps these to the full
constraint bundle internally; and an example library showing constraint
bundles for known produced films so users can orient by analogy.


Risk 5: Conflict Detection False Positives Frustrate Experienced Users

The conflict detection module operates on documented constraint
incompatibilities and will correctly flag most structural conflicts.
However, an experienced writer may have a creative solution to a flagged
conflict that the rule set does not anticipate. If the system requires
conflict resolution before generation proceeds, a false positive blocks
a user who knows exactly what they are doing. The distinction between
a hard incompatibility (no narrative can satisfy both constraints) and
a soft tension (a constraint pair that is challenging but resolvable
through specific creative choices) must be precisely maintained in the
knowledge base and communicated clearly in the user interface.

Mitigation: Classify conflicts as Hard (generation blocked without
resolution), Soft (user warned but generation permitted with
acknowledgment), and Advisory (informational flag only). Hard
classification is reserved for conflicts that are logically irresolvable
(for example, a budget tier that cannot support the minimum requirements
of the selected genre under any narrative approach). Soft and Advisory
conflicts allow the user to proceed with explicit acknowledgment.


Risk 6: No Prior Benchmark Makes Performance Evaluation Non-Standard

There is no prior benchmark for this specific task. Evaluating CASIE's
performance requires developing new evaluation methodology. Proposed
evaluation dimensions: constraint satisfaction rate (percentage of
generated variants that a domain expert independently judges as compliant
with the specified constraint bundle); structural diversity score (average
pairwise Sui Generis score or semantic embedding distance between variants
generated from the same constraint bundle); user utility rating (percentage
of variants that a professional writer or producer judges as actionable
for development); and conflict detection accuracy (precision and recall
of the conflict detection engine against a hand-labeled test set of
constraint bundles with known conflicts). Building this evaluation
framework is a meaningful research contribution in itself and should be
treated as a first-phase deliverable alongside the system implementation.

WORKED EXAMPLE: COMPLETE CONSTRAINT RESOLUTION AND VARIANT
GENERATION FOR A SPECIFIC CONSTRAINT BUNDLE
=========================================================

This section demonstrates the system's behavior on a concrete case to
ground the abstract architecture in specific, verifiable outcomes.

Input Constraint Bundle:
  Genre: Horror (primary), Comedy (secondary modifier - horror-comedy)
  Rating Target: PG-13 (MPA)
  Budget Tier: Micro ($50K and under)
  Distribution Territories: US and India (CBFC)

Layer 1 Conflict Detection Output:
  Conflict 1 (Soft): Horror genre convention (sustained violence,
  graphic imagery) in tension with PG-13 content threshold (no sustained
  intense violence, no graphic gore). Resolution options: (A) shift horror
  convention toward psychological dread and suspense rather than physical
  violence - fully PG-13 compliant; (B) accept PG-13 with horror-lite
  framing; (C) upgrade rating to R and adjust distribution strategy.

  Conflict 2 (Hard): CBFC U/A classification (the approximate Indian
  equivalent audience for PG-13) imposes stricter restrictions on violence
  depiction than MPA PG-13. Some horror content permissible at PG-13 in
  the US will trigger mandatory cuts or A classification in India.
  Resolution required: confirm that horror convention will rely on
  psychological tension and implied threat rather than depicted violence.
  User must acknowledge before generation proceeds.

  Conflict 3 (Advisory): Micro-budget + horror-comedy genre implies
  significant location economy pressure. Comedy typically benefits from
  varied settings for comedic contrast; micro-budget limits to 3 locations
  maximum. Advisory: consider single contained location with internal
  variety (e.g., a large house with distinct rooms) to satisfy both.

User selects Resolution Path: Conflicts 1 and 2 resolved via psychological
horror convention; Conflict 3 acknowledged.

Layer 2 Scope Parameters Generated:
  Max distinct locations: 3
  Max named speaking characters: 5
  VFX complexity: none
  Period setting: contemporary
  Action complexity: no physical stunt work
  Content thresholds: no depicted blood/gore; violence implied not shown;
  language at PG-13 level; no sexual content

Layer 3 Variants Generated:

Variant 1 (Archetype: The Crucible)
Three estranged siblings are forced to spend one night in their recently
deceased parents' house to sign inheritance papers. As the night progresses,
each sibling begins experiencing vivid manifestations of their worst
personal guilt - but always when the other two are not in the room.
By morning, each must confront whether the house is haunted or whether
grief and family dysfunction have made them unreliable witnesses to their
own minds. Horror: psychological dread, no violence. Comedy: dysfunctional
sibling dynamics, absurd guilt manifestations. Locations: one house (three
rooms used distinctly). Cast: three siblings plus two supporting (lawyer,
neighbor). Fully PG-13 and CBFC U/A compliant.

Variant 2 (Archetype: Non-Linear Revelation)
A true crime podcaster investigates the allegedly haunted apartment where
her grandmother died forty years ago, interviewing surviving neighbors in
their current homes while intercutting with the grandmother's final weeks
reconstructed from audio evidence. The horror builds through what the
audio reveals - not violence, but deliberate silence where violence should
be. The comedy emerges from the podcaster's increasingly unhinged
on-air commentary as she loses journalistic detachment. Locations:
podcaster's apartment, two neighbor homes. Cast: podcaster, three elderly
neighbors, grandmother's voice. No depicted violence; fully compliant.

Variant 3 (Archetype: Transformation Arc)
A relentlessly optimistic estate agent must sell a notoriously unsellable
house that everyone in town claims is cursed. Over a single open house
day, a series of increasingly strange prospective buyers arrive - each
one seeming to know something specific about the agent's past. By closing
time, she must decide whether to finally acknowledge the part of her history
she has spent years selling away. Horror: escalating supernatural implication
with no violence. Comedy: estate agent sales patter versus the inexplicable.
Locations: the house (used as one location with internal variety). Cast:
agent plus four prospective buyers (one reveal per act). Fully compliant.

Constraint Satisfaction Reports: all three variants confirmed against all
six scope parameters and both rating system thresholds. No post-generation
flags raised.
